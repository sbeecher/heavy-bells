Ghost Strums

The strumming arm never stops: down, up, down, up. A pattern is only a choice of which swings touch the strings. Tap a swing to make it a strum or a ghost.

1&2&3&4&
The rule: the arm keeps swinging, down on the beat and up in between, whether or not it hits anything.

Why the arm keeps going

Guitar teachers almost all say the same thing: keep the hand swinging even on the beats you skip. Downs land on the numbers, ups on the "and"s, and a skipped strum is just a swing that misses the strings. Stop and restart, they say, and the timing wobbles. Cory Wong calls it a "steady motor". (Guitar World, Guitar Pro)

Timing researchers found something that fits. They asked people to keep a steady beat in two ways: tapping a finger on a table, and drawing circles on the table without stopping, one circle per beat.

Tapping has a clear moment you can point to: the tap. People seem to keep time by running a little clock in their head, one tick per tap. Circling has no such moment. The arm just keeps flowing, and the timing comes out of the smooth movement itself, with no clock needed (Zelaznik, Spencer & Ivry, 2002).

The catch: because a circle has no moment, people found it much harder to land their circles in time with a metronome's clicks (Studenka & Zelaznik, 2011). Then the researchers put a small bump on the table for the finger to brush once a lap. With something to touch, most people's circles started behaving like taps (Studenka, Zelaznik & Balasubramaniam, 2012).

My own guess, which nobody I've found has tested: a strumming arm is both at once. The swing is the smooth circle that keeps going. The strings are the bump you touch. The ghosts are the part of the circle with nothing to touch, and they hold the beat anyway.

Sources: H. Zelaznik, R. Spencer & R. Ivry, “Dissociation of explicit and implicit timing in repetitive tapping and drawing movements”, J. Exp. Psychology: Human Perception and Performance (2002), PDF. B. Studenka & H. Zelaznik, “Circle drawing does not exhibit auditory-motor synchronization”, J. Motor Behavior 43(3), 2011, abstract. B.
