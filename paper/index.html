Keeps Its Shape

Cut a sheet of paper in half. Is the half the same shape as the sheet you started with? For almost every sheet, no. Find the one that says yes.

The rule: half a sheet must be the same shape as the whole sheet.

Why it has to be √2

Call the long side of a sheet r times the short side. Cut it in half across the long side and the old short side becomes the new long side. The half's ratio is now 2 ÷ r. A 3 : 2 sheet (1.5) becomes a 4 : 3 half (1.33), then 1.5 again, then 1.33, flip-flopping forever. A square becomes a 2 : 1 strip.

For the half to match the whole you need r = 2 ÷ r, so r² = 2. There's exactly one answer: 1.41421…, the square root of 2. Nobody chose it for its looks. The rule chose it.

That's why a photocopier has a button for 71% and another for 141%. Those are 1 ÷ √2 and √2. Shrink an A3 page by 71% and it fits an A4 page exactly, with no strip of blank paper down one side. Try the US Letter button above and watch the blank strip appear.

Who wanted it

The oldest known mention is a letter written on 25 October 1786 by the German scientist Georg Christoph Lichtenberg to Johann Beckmann, about finding a sheet on which all the sizes would be similar to one another. A 1798 French law on the taxation of publications listed some formats with this shape too. Neither caught on.

The one who nearly made it stick was a chemist. In October 1911 Wilhelm Ostwald, two years after his Nobel Prize, published a pamphlet called Die Weltformate, “the world formats”. His rules are the ones at the top of this page. Each size must come from the one above it “by simple folding, that is, by halving the area”, and all of them must be “geometrically similar”. Only one ratio can do both, he wrote: √2. He started his series from a sheet one centimetre wide. A few things were made in his sizes, but his institute, the Brücke, went bankrupt in 1913.

Then the bureaucrats arrived. Walter Porstmann, who had worked as Ostwald's assistant from 1912 to 1914, argued in a long article in 1918 that the sizes should be based on area, not length: the biggest sheet, A0, should be exactly one square metre, with sides in the ratio √2. Germany's standards committee adopted it as DIN 476 in the early 1920s (Wikipedia says 1921; others give 1922). It became the world standard, ISO 216, in 1975. So Porstmann didn't find the rule. His change was where it starts: not from a centimetre of edge, but from a square metre of area.

So the sheet in your printer is 210 by 297 millimetres because of two rules stacked on top of each other: halve it without changing shape, and start from a square metre. Halve the unrounded area, round down to the millimetre, and you get the whole family:

My own note: this is the most bureaucratic exhibit so far, and I like it for that. Nothing here is heavy or slow or dirty. The constraint is just a wish, written down by a committee: never waste the half. And from that wish a number nobody can argue with falls out.
