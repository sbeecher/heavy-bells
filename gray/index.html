Only One Switch

New Jersey, 1947. At Bell Labs, Frank Gray is helping turn a voice into pulses, so a voice can travel down a line as numbers. Over and over, many times a second, a vacuum tube measures how loud the voice is and writes it down as a binary number. Inside the tube, a beam of electrons is aimed at a metal plate punched with holes, one row of holes for each loudness. The holes it passes through are the number.

The trouble is that a beam wobbles. Aim it at one row and it drifts a hair toward the next. In plain binary, a hair can be a disaster. Gray wrote that it “may result in a coding error which is far greater than the beam deflection error.” Miss by a whisker, and the number that comes out can say quiet when the voice was loud.

Below is the same trouble in a form you can watch: a dial instead of a beam. Each ring is one digit, read by a little contact, and the contacts are never quite lined up. That's the wobble. Turn it and watch the dial lie.

On the Counting setting, every so often the machine reads a number nowhere near the dial: 15 when it's at 7, say. Going from 7 to 8 on four switches, all four flip at once (0111 to 1000), and real switches never flip at exactly the same instant. Caught halfway, the machine reads a mix of old and new: any of sixteen numbers.

Now press Gray's code.

The rule: neighbouring positions may differ in only one switch.

The fix was to count differently

Gray didn't steady the beam. He reordered the numbers. In his order, each step up or down changes exactly one digit, so a reading caught mid-step can only be the old number or the new one. A wobble stays a wobble. His patent's aim was a code whose error “is never greater than the beam deflection error.”

He called it the reflected binary code and noted it had “as yet no recognized name”. The patent was filed in 1947 and granted in 1953, and everyone started calling it Gray code. The tube itself was built by Raymond W. Sears, working with Gray and William M. Goodall. Today the same trick sits inside the dials that tell machines where their shafts and arms are, the use the toy above shows.

He wasn't first. In the 1870s the French telegraph engineer Émile Baudot ordered the letters on his printing wheel the same way. Louis Gros described the order in 1872, as the answer to an old puzzle called the Chinese rings.

The “reflected” part is how you build it. Write the list for one switch: 0, 1. Write it again backwards, as if in a mirror, and put a 1 in front of the mirrored half: 00, 01, 11, 10. Mirror again for three switches, and so on. Each ring on the disc is the same mirror trick at a bigger size.

My own note: the bells in exhibit 03 have a cousin of this rule. Each bell may move only one place per change, because it's too heavy to do more. Here, only one switch may move at all, because the switches can't agree on when.

Cousin Heavy Bells, and closer than my note above. The same idea, with orders instead of switches: list every order of some bells so that each one differs from the last by swapping only two neighbours. English change ringers were doing this (“plain changes”) by about 1621; mathematicians rediscovered it in the early 1960s as the Steinhaus–Johnson–Trotter algorithm. (Wikipedia: Steinhaus–Johnson–Trotter algorithm)

Not sure Why Frank Gray's name stuck. Baudot and Gros used the same order decades before him. Wikipedia gives the history but not the reason, and I don't know it. (One thing I have now checked myself: the patent really does say “as yet no recognized name”.)
