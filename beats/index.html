Even Beats

A particle accelerator ticks sixty times a second. Some of its parts must only fire on a few of those ticks. Which few? It sounds like nothing. It turns out to be the tresillo.

The problem

In the early 2000s the Spallation Neutron Source was being built at Oak Ridge, Tennessee: an accelerator that fires pulses of protons at a metal target to knock neutrons loose. It runs on a 60 Hz beat, so every second is cut into 60 slots, and its timing repeats every 10 seconds, 600 slots in all.

Not everything fires on every slot. While the machine is being tuned, the beam can run as slowly as once every ten seconds, while other parts run at fractions of 60: the source at 10 a second, the radio-frequency system at 20, the kickers at 30. Each part needs to be told which slots are its own, and the patterns had to come out “smooth”.

Say a part needs 3 shots in every 8 slots. The lazy answer is “every other slot until you run out”. Press it below and listen: three quick shots, then a long gap. That's a lump, not a rhythm.

The rule: spread the beats as evenly as the steps allow. When they don't divide evenly, share out the leftovers the way Euclid's algorithm shares out remainders.

The fix

E. Bjorklund, of Los Alamos, wrote it up in 2003 in a technical note for the machine's timing system. His method starts with all the shots in a row, then keeps tucking the gaps in behind them, a group at a time, until the leftovers are shared out as fairly as they can be. For 3 shots in 8 slots it gives x · · x · · x ·.

Two years later the computer scientist Godfried Toussaint noticed two things. Bjorklund's steps are the same steps as Euclid's algorithm, from around 300 BC. And the patterns it makes are rhythms people already dance to. x · · x · · x · is the Cuban tresillo. Five in eight is the cinquillo. The engineers weren't trying to make music. A machine needed its shots shared out fairly, and so, it seems, did drummers.

Bjorklund wasn't first to spread things out evenly, either. Computer screens do it every time they draw a slanting line out of square pixels: Jack Bresenham's line-drawing method (1965) turns out to follow Euclid's algorithm too. Calendar-makers spreading leap days across years face the same problem. And music theorists got there first in music, for scales rather than rhythms: John Clough and Jack Douthett called them “maximally even sets” in 1991. What was new in 2005 was the drums.
